# AI coding contest day 16: WarehouseRobot. Gemini wins 9 of 10.

The sixteenth challenge is a load-dependent vehicle-routing puzzle. A robot starts at a depot at `(0, 0)` on an integer Manhattan grid and must collect every item from a list of bins, returning to the depot at the end of every trip to unload. The robot's speed depends on its current load: 10 m/min unladen, dropping by 1 m/min for every 10 kg carried, and stuck at ≥ 100 kg. Each trip therefore juggles two coupled decisions: which items to pack (each trip's total weight must stay below 100 kg) and the visit order within a trip (heavy items late minimises the slow legs, but the bin geometry can flip that). The bot computes the whole schedule offline and submits a list of trips; the server simulates and reports total elapsed time. Lowest time wins.

The format is 10 solo rounds played serially, with warehouse sizes growing from `30 × 30` m to `200 × 200` m and item counts from 20 to 250. Item placements, weights, and counts are server-generated per round. The bot is told the round's dimensions and item list at round start and adapts at runtime; the per-round schedule is not announced in advance.

Per-round ranking → tournament points: 1st = 10, 2nd = 7, 3rd = 5, 4th = 3, 5th = 1, 6th and below = 0. Ties on `total_time` break by earliest submission timestamp at the server. Each bot has a 30-second wall-clock budget per round.

Bots were generated by sending the prompt to each model via OpenRouter in a single chat completion, except for five model bots authored via direct chat. **MiMo (V2.5-Pro) is DNF.** Three regeneration attempts each terminated with `finish_reason=length` and 65,535 reasoning tokens, with the model emitting zero output tokens each time. By the rule, three ICoTL attempts is the cutoff; MiMo never produced a bot file. This is MiMo's third consecutive challenge as DNF in generation.

## Gemini: large-neighbourhood search with simulated annealing

Gemini (Pro 3.1, 227 lines) is the only bot that converts a fully-spent 30-second budget into iterative search wins. Its `solve_round` does:

1. **Initial solution**: farthest-insertion. Items are sorted by Manhattan distance from the depot descending, then inserted one at a time greedily into the trip-position that minimises the marginal time increase. New trips are spawned when no insertion is cheaper than starting fresh.
2. **Large-neighbourhood search loop**, run with a 28.5-second budget: each iteration removes 1 to 20 items at random from the current schedule, then re-inserts them greedily one at a time using the same marginal-cost rule. Time changes accept or reject the new schedule.
3. **Simulated-annealing acceptance**: when the new schedule is worse, it's accepted with probability `exp(−Δ / T)` where the temperature `T` decays linearly from 2.0 to 0 across the round. Lets the search escape local minima early but tightens late.

The destruction-and-repair pattern is exactly the structure that beats greedy heuristics on capacitated VRP. Because each iteration is cheap (an LNS pass on 250 items takes a few milliseconds), Gemini fits hundreds to thousands of LNS iterations per round into the budget. The 3–5% time margin over the next-best bot is consistent with what a well-tuned LNS+SA buys over a one-shot greedy.

Gemini's 12,844-minute total is also the lowest in the field, despite tying R1 with DeepSeek and losing the timestamp tiebreak.

## Claude: Held-Karp + Clarke-Wright + multi-pass local search

Claude (Opus 4.7, 677 lines) is the most engineered bot. The pipeline:

1. **Clarke-Wright savings algorithm** to seed the initial trip set: build single-item trips, then iteratively merge pairs whose merged time is less than the sum of the two single-trip times.
2. **Held-Karp dynamic programming** for trips of ≤ 11 items: exact bitmask DP that finds the optimal visit order for the items in a given trip.
3. **Local-search passes**: `relocate_pass` (move an item from one trip to another), `swap_pass` (swap two items across trips), `extract_pass` (pull an item out into its own trip), `merge_pass` (merge two trips).

Each pass runs in a deadline-checked loop. Across 10 rounds, Claude posts the second-best time on every round it wasn't beaten on, and consistently finishes 2nd or 3rd. Total: 13,420 minutes, about 4.5% above Gemini's. **Zero first-place finishes.**

The structure tells the story: every component of Claude's solver is correct and locally-optimal. Held-Karp gives exact intra-trip ordering for small trips, the four local-search passes cover every move type the textbook lists. But the search is *deterministic and convex*: it descends to a local minimum and stops. There's no equivalent of Gemini's destruction-and-repair to climb out. On a packing problem with hundreds of items and dozens of trips, that's the difference between 1st and 2nd.

This is the same pattern Claude has shown on past solo challenges: a heavily-engineered, correct, locally-optimal solver that loses the title to a simpler bot doing iterative re-search.

## ChatGPT: heap-driven Clarke-Wright with exact savings

ChatGPT (GPT 5.5, 385 lines) implements a stronger Clarke-Wright variant: instead of approximating savings with the standard distance-based formula, it computes savings by *simulating* the merged trip exactly with the load-dependent speed function and pushes the saving into a max-heap. Each merge step pops the best saving, validates it (the entry may be stale due to earlier merges), and applies it.

After the merge phase, ChatGPT runs an adjacent-swap pass on each trip, plus a broader pair-swap for trips of ≤ 55 items. Total time 13,417 minutes, within 25 minutes of Claude across 10 rounds. ChatGPT never wins a round but consistently lands 3rd or 4th. The exact-savings approach is strictly stronger than vanilla Clarke-Wright on this problem (the load-dependent speed makes textbook savings inaccurate), but without an LNS-style escape it shares Claude's ceiling.

## DeepSeek: ambitious, then time-budgeted out

DeepSeek (V4-Pro, 263 lines) ran a 2-opt-plus-iterative-improve solver with a 28-second per-round budget. On R1 it tied Gemini at the optimum; on R2–R7 it placed 2nd or 3rd. Its `t=` measurements show the budget exhausted on every round (28.0–29.8 s). At R8 (170 × 170 m, 180 items) the solver overran the 30-second deadline, the server emitted `INVALID timeout`, and DeepSeek's process disconnected. R9 and R10 then read as immediate EOF.

Banked 42 points before disconnecting, ranking 4th overall. Without the R8 overrun it would likely have finished 3rd or higher.

## The middle: Muse, GLM

**Muse (Spark, 140 lines)** caps trip weight at 79 kg (not 99). The bot's source comments explain the choice as "keep speed ≥ 3 m/min, heavier trips are slower than splitting." It then sorts by polar angle around the depot, packs greedily, and runs a small 2-opt pass on trips of ≤ 10 items. The 79 kg cap is conservative; the optimal balance for the speed function is around 90 kg per trip, so Muse uses more trips than necessary. Scores 11 points by hitting 4th–5th place on R6–R10 when DeepSeek's DNFs and the slowness of Grok/Kimi opened up the lower top-5 slots.

**GLM (5.1, 514 lines)** is one of the longest bots in the field but lands 6th on points. Solid 5th-place finisher on R1–R5, then drops behind Muse on R6–R10. 8 points total. Its solver completes correctly on every round but its time gap to the leaders grows with N, a sign that the algorithm doesn't scale as well as Gemini's LNS.

## The tail: Grok, Kimi, Nemotron

**Grok (Expert 4.3 Beta, 293 lines)** and **Kimi (K2.6, 320 lines)** both finish all 10 rounds with valid submissions but never crack the top 5 on time. Their per-round times are 20–35% above Gemini's on the larger rounds, putting them outside the points zone. Both correctly understand the load-aware speed function and produce non-overlapping trip plans, but their search is shallow (greedy-style heuristics that don't iterate). Total 0 tournament points each, despite never crashing. Grok's full-run total of 16,581 minutes is 29% above Gemini's; Kimi's 17,049 is 33% above.

**Nemotron (3 Super, 122 lines)** has a parser off-by-one bug at line 46:

```python
if len(it_parts) != 4:
    continue
```

`ITEM` lines have 5 tokens (`ITEM <idx> <x> <y> <weight>`), not 4. The check skips every item line, leaving the bot's `items` list permanently empty. The bot then plans 0 trips and submits a bare `END`, which the server validates as `missing_item_0` (no items covered). The bug fires on every one of the 10 rounds. Total 0 points; the bot never produced a single valid trip.

## Why this challenge differentiated

R1 (30 × 30 m, 20 items, 3 trips) was tractable enough that two bots independently found the optimum at 54.68 minutes. That's the kind of small-board converged result where any bot with a competent solver hits the same answer and the title goes to whoever's first on the wire. From R2 onward the search space expanded fast enough that no bot found the true optimum, and the standings sorted by who got closer.

The shape of the field is unusual: zero same-time ties past R1, a 4–5% margin separating 1st from 2nd on every round, and a clean ordering from "spends the budget on iterative re-search" (Gemini) down through "runs a one-shot heuristic and stops" (Claude / ChatGPT / DeepSeek's pre-disconnect performance) to "doesn't iterate at all" (Grok, Kimi).

## The verdict

Three populations:

- **Iterative-search winner (Gemini, 97 points)**: LNS + simulated annealing, 28.5 s of search per round. Wins 9 of 10 by ~4% margins. Shows that on a routing problem with this much structure, the answer that wins is the one that keeps searching.
- **Engineered cluster (Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek)**: every textbook component of capacitated VRP, deterministic local search, no global escape. 2nd–4th by ≤ 5% on every round, but 0 first-place finishes between them on the rounds where both top tiers played.
- **Tail (Muse, GLM, Grok, Kimi)**: correct solvers, weaker heuristics, no iterative refinement. Score sporadically or not at all.

Plus two failures that have nothing to do with algorithm: Nemotron's `len != 4` bug, and MiMo's third-in-a-row generation collapse.

Gemini's win is real and earned. The decisive design choice, destroy-and-repair LNS with SA, is what every commercial VRP solver does, just with more knobs. Claude's 677 lines of careful textbook engineering still couldn't catch a 227-line bot doing the right meta-algorithm.
